# Pharmacovigilance Reporting Procedure

## 0.1 Purpose
This procedure sets out how Pharma A receives, records, assesses and forwards information about adverse events that reaches the company through medical information, so that every event is reported to the regulators on time and no event is lost because a doctor mentioned it in passing. It applies the principles of ICH E2D (post-approval safety data management) and ICH E2B(R3) (the structure of an individual case safety report), and the national rules that follow from them.

The procedure covers CardioNex (cardionexib). The assistant that answers doctors follows the same rules for any other company product whose procedure refers to this one.

## 0.2 Scope
This procedure applies to every contact through which a health professional or a consumer can tell the company about a patient: the doctor chat, email to the medical information address, and calls to the call center. It applies to all messages, including those that ask a different question. A doctor who asks about a dose and, in the same message, says the patient fainted has reported an adverse event.

This procedure does not cover clinical trial safety reporting, which follows the protocol and the sponsor's trial procedure, or the handling of product quality complaints, which follows the quality complaints procedure. A message that holds both an adverse event and a quality complaint is handled under both procedures.

## 0.3 Roles and responsibilities
- **The medical information assistant** recognises a possible adverse event or special situation, sends the whole message to the drug safety queue, tells the reporter what happens next, and gives no medical advice about the event. It never decides that something is "not really" an adverse event.
- **The medical information specialist** handles messages the assistant routes for review, answers the medical question after the safety hand-over, and asks the reporter for any missing fact in the same reply.
- **The pharmacovigilance reviewer** is a trained person on the drug safety team. The reviewer checks the case, codes it, assesses seriousness, expectedness and causality, files the report, and owns follow-up.
- **The qualified person for pharmacovigilance** is responsible for the safety system as a whole, signs off this procedure and answers to the regulator for late or missing reports.
- **The quality assurance lead** audits the system each year and samples cases each month (see 6.1).

## 0.4 Definitions
- **Adverse event (AE):** any untoward medical occurrence in a patient who was given a medicine, whether or not it is thought to be caused by the medicine. A lab abnormality that the doctor reports as a problem, a symptom, a diagnosis and a death are all adverse events.
- **Adverse drug reaction (ADR):** an adverse event for which a causal link with the medicine is at least a reasonable possibility. A reporter's statement that the medicine "may have caused it" is enough to treat the event as a reaction.
- **Serious:** the event results in death, is life-threatening, needs or lengthens a hospital stay, causes lasting or significant disability, is a birth defect, or is another medically important event that may need treatment to prevent one of these. Seriousness describes the outcome, not how bad the symptom felt. Severity (mild, moderate, severe) is a different scale and does not decide seriousness.
- **Unexpected:** an event whose nature or severity is not listed in the current prescribing information. Dizziness at the rate in section 6.1 of the label is expected. An event the label does not mention is unexpected. A listed event that is worse than the label describes is also unexpected.
- **Special situation:** a use of the medicine that must be recorded even when no adverse event follows: exposure during pregnancy or breast-feeding, overdose, misuse, abuse, medication error, lack of efficacy, off-label use, and occupational exposure (see 3.1 to 3.5).
- **Individual case safety report (ICSR):** the record of one patient's event, with the four minimum criteria in 2.1.
- **MedDRA:** the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, the coding dictionary used in every ICSR. This dataset uses a fictional MedDRA-style dictionary with invented codes for teaching.
- **Awareness date:** the day any employee of the company, or a contractor acting for it, first receives information that holds the four minimum criteria.

## 1.1 Who reports and when
Every adverse event about a CardioNex patient goes to the drug safety team within **24 hours** of first contact. A human pharmacovigilance reviewer files the report. The assistant never files it.

## 1.2 What the report holds
The report holds the product, the event, the date and the reporter's role. It never holds a patient name, a patient record number or a doctor's email address.

## 1.3 The four facts a report needs
A report is valid only when it has an identifiable patient (age or sex is enough), an identifiable reporter, the medicine and the event. A missing fact is asked for, never guessed.

## 1.4 Serious events
Death, a threat to life, a hospital stay, lasting disability or a birth defect make an event serious. A serious event is flagged at the top of the report so the reviewer sees it first.

## 1.5 What the desk tells the reporter
The desk thanks the reporter, says the event goes to the drug safety team, and gives no medical advice about the event.

## 2.1 The four minimum criteria
A report is a valid ICSR when it holds all four of the following. Sections 1.3 and 2.1 describe the same four facts.

| Criterion | What is enough | What is not enough |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Identifiable reporter | A health professional's role and a way to reach them (the doctor's practice, an email or a call-back number held on the contact record) | "A colleague told me", or a message with no sender |
| Identifiable patient | One or more of: age, age group, sex, initials, a patient number or "my patient". The patient need not be named | "A few of my patients", "some people", a group with no individual |
| Suspect product | The company product, by brand or generic name, even when the dose or lot is unknown | "My heart failure drug", with no way to tell which one |
| Adverse event | A symptom, sign, diagnosis, lab abnormality or outcome that the reporter links in time with the medicine | A question about a side effect in general, or a rate in a trial |

When one or more of the four is missing, the case is still recorded. It is not valid yet, and the clock in 2.2 does not start. The specialist asks for the missing fact in the same reply (see 2.5). The assistant never guesses a missing fact, and never fills it from the contact record of a different patient.

An event is not an adverse event merely because the words "side effect" or "reaction" appear in the message. A doctor who asks what the rate of dizziness was in the clinical trial, or whether hyperkalaemia is a contraindication, has asked a medical question about the label and has not reported an event. The assistant answers it from the label and cites the section. The reverse also holds: a doctor who never uses those words but writes that the patient "went to the emergency room after her lips swelled" has reported an event.

## 2.2 Day 0 and the clock
Day 0 is the awareness date, the first calendar day on which the four criteria in 2.1 are all held by anyone at the company or its contractors. The day the message arrives is day 0 when the message already holds all four. When a later reply supplies the last missing criterion, the day of that reply is day 0.

Two clocks run from the message. Section 1.1 requires the whole message to reach the drug safety team within 24 hours of first contact, even when it is incomplete. The regulatory clock in 2.3 starts at day 0 and is counted in calendar days, including weekends and holidays. A message that arrives on a Friday evening is on the queue by Saturday evening, and day 0 is Friday if it was complete.

Information received by a sales representative, a call center agent, a field medical scientist or a contractor counts as received by the company. Each of them forwards it as an adverse event on the day they get it.

## 2.3 Expedited reporting timelines
- A serious, unexpected adverse drug reaction is reported to the regulator within **15 calendar days** of day 0. This is an expedited report.
- A serious reaction that is listed in the label (expected) is reported in the same 15-calendar-day expedited timeline in the United States when the product is marketed under an application that requires it, and otherwise in the periodic report. The reviewer applies the rule in the regional annex for the country of the reporter.
- A fatal or life-threatening reaction is escalated the same working day to the qualified person, who decides whether to file before the 15 days have run. Filing early is always allowed.
- A non-serious reaction, listed or not, is not expedited. It goes to the regulator in the periodic safety report.
- When new information changes seriousness, for example a patient who was treated in the emergency room is later admitted, the 15 days restart from the day the new information arrives, and the case is reported again as a follow-up version.

A report is late when it passes these limits. A late report is a deviation and is recorded with its cause under 6.1. The cause is never "the message was incomplete" if the 24-hour hand-over in 1.1 was missed.

## 2.4 Seriousness and expectedness
The reviewer, never the assistant, assesses seriousness, expectedness and causality. The assistant only flags a case as possibly serious, as 1.4 says, so that the reviewer reads it first. The flag uses the reporter's words: a hospital stay, an emergency room visit that ended in admission, a death, a threat to life, lasting disability or a birth defect. The reviewer decides whether an emergency room visit was a medically important event.

Expectedness is judged against the label in force on the day the reporter sent the message, not the label as it stands when the case is closed. Where two labels are in force in two countries, the reviewer uses the label of the country of the reporter.

## 2.5 Follow-up
When a case is not valid, or when key facts are missing, the specialist asks for them. The reply to the reporter asks plainly for what is missing: the patient's age and sex, the dose and start date of the medicine, the date the event began, the outcome and any other medicines. It does not ask for the patient's name, record number or date of birth. Section 1.2 forbids them in the report.

The company makes at least **two** attempts to obtain follow-up information on a serious case, the second after about seven calendar days, and records each attempt, its date and its channel. The company stops after the second attempt when there is no answer, and notes in the case that the reporter did not respond. A reporter who answers late is still taken: the late answer is added as a follow-up. For a death, the company also asks for the cause of death and whether an autopsy was done.

Follow-up questions are written once, to the reporter, and are never put to the patient. A reporter who is a consumer is asked for permission to contact their doctor.

## 3.1 Special situations: exposure during pregnancy and breast-feeding
Any report that a woman took the company product while pregnant, or while breast-feeding, is recorded, even if the baby and the mother are well. A male patient whose partner became pregnant is also recorded. A pregnancy exposure is reported with the date of the last menstrual period or the expected delivery date when known.

The reviewer opens a pregnancy case and follows it until the outcome is known: live birth, miscarriage, termination or stillbirth. The company asks the reporter for the outcome at about the expected delivery date, and again about one month after it, so that a birth defect is not missed. A birth defect, a miscarriage and a fetal death after exposure are serious events and follow 2.3.

The assistant does not tell the reporter whether to continue the medicine. Where the label carries a warning on pregnancy, the specialist may quote the label and cite the section, and tells the reporter that the treating doctor decides.

## 3.2 Special situations: overdose
An overdose is a dose above the highest dose in the label, taken on purpose or by accident. An overdose with symptoms is an adverse event with the symptoms coded. An overdose without symptoms is still recorded as a special situation and is reported when it is serious or when the regional annex requires it. The reviewer records the dose taken, the dose that was intended, the time, any treatment given and whether it was an attempt at self-harm. The assistant never gives treatment advice and refers the doctor to the label's overdosage section and to a poison centre.

## 3.3 Special situations: medication error
A medication error is a mistake in prescribing, dispensing or taking the medicine: a wrong dose, a wrong patient, a wrong drug of similar name, a missed washout, a drug given together with a contraindicated medicine. The report records the type of error, whether it reached the patient, and whether it caused harm. An error that reached the patient and caused harm is an adverse event. An error that reached the patient without harm is a special situation. An error that was caught before it reached the patient (a "near miss") is recorded in the error log and reviewed by the reviewer, who decides whether it needs an ICSR.

## 3.4 Special situations: lack of efficacy
A report that the medicine did not work is recorded when it holds the four criteria. It is reported as an event when the patient had a worsening of the condition the medicine is meant to prevent, for example a hospital stay for heart failure in a patient on CardioNex. A lack of efficacy that is suspected to come from a drug interaction, a counterfeit product or a dosing error is escalated to the reviewer at once.

## 3.5 Special situations: off-label use and misuse
Use for a condition, in a group or at a dose that the label does not cover is off-label use. It is recorded when the reporter links it to an event, or when the reporter says the patient took the medicine. A doctor who only asks whether the product is approved for a use has asked a medical question. The specialist answers from the label, and does not record a special situation. Misuse, abuse and occupational exposure are recorded in the same way.

## 4.1 MedDRA coding conventions
The reviewer codes each event with a MedDRA lowest level term (LLT) that is as close as possible to the reporter's words, and the coded term is stored with its preferred term (PT). The rules are these:
- Code what the reporter said, not what the reviewer thinks happened. A reporter who writes "K of 6.2" is coded to the term for high potassium and not to cardiac arrhythmia.
- Code a diagnosis when the reporter gives one, and do not also code the symptoms that belong to it. Code symptoms when no diagnosis is given.
- Code each distinct event once. A patient who "fainted and hit her head" has two events: loss of consciousness and head injury.
- Code lab results as events only when the reporter calls them a problem, or when they led to an action such as stopping the medicine.
- Do not code the indication, the medical history or the reason the medicine was started, unless it worsened.
- For a death, code the cause when it is known, and record death as the outcome. "Death" alone is coded only when the cause is not known.
- Code a medication error and the resulting event as separate terms.
- Use the version of the dictionary in force on the date of coding, and record the version. Recoding after a dictionary upgrade creates a new version of the case.

## 4.2 Duplicate detection
The same event can reach the company from the doctor, from the doctor's pharmacist, from a sales representative and from the regulator. A duplicate must not be reported twice. Before the reviewer opens a case, the system searches for an existing case with the same suspect product and either the same reporter and event date, or the same patient identifiers (age, sex and initials or patient number) and the same event term.

When the search finds a match, the reviewer compares the two and chooses one of three actions: merge them as one case with two sources, keep both as separate cases for two patients, or record the second as a follow-up to the first. A message that the queue delivers twice, as when a retry sends the same email again, is detected by its message identifier and is dropped before it creates a case. The system never merges on the strength of age and sex alone.

## 5.1 Reconciliation with medical information
Every month the drug safety team and medical information compare their records. Each medical information contact that holds an adverse event must be on the safety database, and every case on the safety database that began as a medical information contact must be on the medical information log. Differences are investigated within five working days. A contact that was not sent to the safety queue is sent at once, and the missed 24-hour hand-over is recorded as a deviation.

The same reconciliation covers the call center log, the email inbox, the sales representative notes and the answers sent by the assistant. A note in the notes of a representative that says "patient had a reaction" counts as a report on the day it was written.

The medical question and the safety report are answered separately. The specialist answers the medical question from the label. The reply cites the label section and never says that the event was or was not caused by the product.

## 6.1 Quality control
Each month the quality assurance lead draws a random sample of at least 5% of the cases, and at least 10 cases, from all channels, and checks them for these points: the four criteria are correctly recorded, seriousness is correct, the coding matches the reporter's words, the case was forwarded within 24 hours, and the report went to the regulator on time. A separate sample of medical information messages that were not sent to the safety queue is read to check that none of them held an event. A missed event is a critical finding and the cause is investigated.

The quality plan sets these targets: all expedited reports on time, 100% of events with the 24-hour hand-over, and a coding agreement of at least 95% between the reviewer and the checker. A finding is closed with a corrective action and a date. Deviations from this procedure are recorded, with their cause, and reviewed at the quarterly safety meeting.

## 6.2 Records retention
Safety case records, including the original message, the coded terms, the follow-up attempts and the files sent to the regulator, are kept for the life of the product on the market and for at least 10 years after it is withdrawn. Records are kept in the safety database and are not changed afterward. A correction is added as a new version, with the date and the reason. Access is limited to the drug safety team, the qualified person and auditors.

The report holds no patient name, record number or doctor's email address (section 1.2). The original message may hold them and is kept in the restricted case file. The assistant's own log of the conversation is kept under the same rules and is not used for other purposes.
